# Independent-cohort metric specification

## Why the old paired metrics do not answer this design

The two cohorts contain different participants. Therefore ordinary person-level differences, Bland–Altman limits, temporal trace correlation, and individual map agreement are not identifiable. Inventing a pairing between rows would understate uncertainty and produce meaningless agreement estimates.

The primary analysis unit is the participant. For maps, uncertainty is generated by resampling participants within each cohort.

## Metric hierarchy

### 1. Protocol comparability — mandatory gate

| Item | Calculation | Importance | Failure action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Common stimuli | Exact ID/version coverage by cohort | Different faces can create larger map shifts than the measurement workflow. | Restrict to shared stimuli or stop. |
| Common task/exposure | One task and duration version per stimulus | Instruction and viewing-time differences alter gaze. | Restrict or model explicitly. |
| Common transform | One normalized stimulus transform version | Pixel coordinates are not comparable across displays. | Reprocess before analysis. |
| Common QC | Same exclusion rule version | Different filtering can manufacture a quality difference. | Rerun with harmonized QC. |

### 2. Covariate balance — confounding diagnostic

Standardized mean difference:

`SMD = (mean_webcam - mean_professional) / pooled_SD`

For a binary variable, the mean is its prevalence. SMD expresses balance without making it depend strongly on sample size. `|SMD| < 0.10` is a review convention, not a formal pass/fail law and not evidence of randomization.

Participant characteristics and acquisition context are labeled separately. Display/lighting differences are part of the real workflow and should not automatically be adjusted away.

### 3. Technical quality — primary endpoints

| Endpoint | Participant-level definition | Unit / direction | Why separate? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calibration accuracy | Mean angular distance between gaze and known target | degrees; lower | Detects systematic spatial error. |
| RMS precision | RMS consecutive displacement during stable target viewing | degrees; lower | Detects jitter even when mean location is correct. |
| Data loss | invalid expected samples / all expected samples | proportion; lower | Missingness can change maps and selection. |
| Valid-trial share | valid trials / expected trials | proportion; higher | Detects attrition hidden by survivor averages. |

For each endpoint, report:

- Webcam and professional distributions;
- mean difference `Webcam − Professional`;
- Hedges' g as a standardized descriptive effect;
- Welch 90% CI for the independent-sample difference;
- equivalence decision against a preregistered symmetric margin.

Decision rule:

- `similar_within_margin`: full 90% CI inside `[-margin, +margin]`;
- `meaningfully_different`: full 90% CI beyond one margin;
- `inconclusive`: all other cases.

This is a TOST-style equivalence interpretation. A nonsignificant zero-difference test is not evidence of similarity.

### 4. Group attention map — primary group endpoint

For every participant and stimulus, create a dwell-duration histogram on one fixed normalized grid. Aggregate selected participant histograms and apply the same Gaussian smoothing to each cohort map.

Histogram intersection:

`SIM(P, Q) = sum(min(P_i, Q_i))`, after both maps sum to 1.

SIM ranges from 0 to 1 and has an intuitive “shared density” interpretation. It is the primary map metric in the simple dashboard. Other metrics such as correlation, Jensen–Shannon distance, or earth mover's distance can be sensitivity analyses because saliency metrics reward different properties.

Repeat `B` times per stimulus:

1. split Webcam participants at random; calculate `SIM(W1, W2)`;
2. split professional participants; calculate `SIM(P1, P2)`;
3. calculate `SIM(W1, P1)`;
4. calculate `gap = cross_SIM - min(within_Webcam_SIM, within_professional_SIM)`.

Use the 5th and 95th percentiles for the 90% interval. If the lower gap bound is above `-margin`, cross-domain similarity is noninferior to the lower within-cohort benchmark by the declared margin. This asks a practical question: is the cross-domain loss larger than ordinary participant-sampling instability?

### 5. AOI dwell profile — interpretable secondary endpoint

For each participant and stimulus:

`AOI dwell share = dwell duration inside AOI / total valid dwell duration`

AOIs must be versioned and stimulus-aligned. Include an `other` category so the vector sums to 1. Report cohort means and participant-level 95% CIs. AOIs are secondary because large boxes can hide meaningful shifts within a region.

### 6. Classifier two-sample diagnostic — “same domain?”

Use a regularized logistic regression with repeated stratified five-fold cross-validation. Never include administrative identifiers that trivially reveal cohort.

Run two models:

- technical quality: accuracy, precision, data loss, valid-trial share;
- attention pattern: stimulus-by-AOI dwell shares.

Report out-of-fold AUC and a stratified participant-bootstrap 95% interval:

- `AUC = 0.50`: chance discrimination;
- higher AUC: more separable feature distributions.

Thresholds in the mock config are illustrative. Low AUC does not prove equality; high AUC localizes a distribution shift to the chosen feature set but does not by itself determine whether the scientific endpoint is unusable.

## Why there is no single overall score

A device can have worse precision and loss yet preserve the rank/order of group facial attention. Conversely, attractive group heatmaps can hide many unusable participants. Combining these facts into one score would require externally validated weights and could conceal the exact failure that matters to clinicians.

The decision table therefore keeps protocol, technical quality, group map repeatability, technical-domain AUC, and attention-domain AUC separate.

## Deferred or optional analyses

- Maximum mean discrepancy or energy distance: useful multivariate sensitivity analyses, but less immediately interpretable than AUC for the primary dashboard.
- Propensity weighting: appropriate only for prespecified measured participant covariates with adequate overlap; it cannot remove unmeasured confounding.
- Individual agreement/Bland–Altman: requires the same people measured by both workflows.
- Temporal alignment: requires synchronized traces on the same viewing episode.
- Fairness/subgroup conclusions: require adequate subgroup counts, justified variables, and multiplicity control.
